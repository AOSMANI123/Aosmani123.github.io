A policy desk for the five-layer AI stack

Policymakers don't need another explainer of how GPUs work. They need to know which live question lands on which layer of the AI economy, what the company on the record has already said about it, and what a staffer can read in ninety seconds. This desk does that for all five layers, and an agent pipeline keeps it current without ever publishing on its own.

Built October 2026Audience Policy & government-affairs commsOutputs Brief · One-pager · LinkedInPipeline Monitor → Classify → Draft → Source-check → Human

Why this exists

In March 2026 NVIDIA's CEO described AI as a five-layer cake: energy, chips, infrastructure, models, applications. It is a good frame for engineers. It is an even better frame for Washington, because almost every live policy fight in 2026 sits cleanly on one layer, and the people arguing about them rarely say which.

A policy marketing team's job is to translate priorities into content a non-technical audience can use, fast, and without putting words in the company's mouth. So the test I set myself: can I build a desk that produces a sourced brief, a one-pager and a social post for any layer, on demand, with every claim traceable, and with a human holding the only publish button?

Short version. Pick a layer below. You get the live U.S. policy questions on that layer, the company's published position with links to the primary document, a 150-word brief written for a Hill staffer, a one-pager skeleton with third-party validators, and a LinkedIn draft. Where no published position exists, the desk says so instead of inventing one. Everything here is built from public sources and is not affiliated with NVIDIA.

      How the content gets made

      This is a slimmed version of a seven-agent system I run in my day job for executive engagements, pointed at policy sources instead of meeting requests. Four agents do the work. A person makes the decision.

      01 · MonitorWatch the primary sources

      Federal Register and BIS rules, congress.gov bill actions, FERC dockets, state legislature feeds, and the company's own blog and SEC filings. Nothing from secondary press enters as a fact.

      02 · ClassifyPut it on a layer

      Each item is tagged with one of the five layers and a type: legislative, regulatory, or geopolitical. Ambiguous items go to a person, not to a coin flip.

      03 · DraftWrite the three formats

      Brief, one-pager, LinkedIn post. The drafter is given only the company's published materials on that layer and a style guide; it is not allowed to extend a position.

      04 · Source-checkEvery claim gets a link or the draft stops

      A second agent reads the draft cold and attaches a primary source to each factual sentence. Any sentence it can't source is flagged, and a flagged draft cannot move forward.

      05 · HumanApprove, edit, or kill

      The only publish action in the system is a person's. The log records what was changed and why, so the drafter's prompt improves over time.

      What the pipeline will never publish on its own

      • RULE 1Anything that states or implies the company's position on a pending bill or rule, unless a published document says it in those words.
      • RULE 2Anything touching export controls, China, or tariffs. These drafts are produced for a person to read, never queued.
      • RULE 3Anything quoting an executive. Quotes are pasted from the source by a person, with the link.
      • RULE 4Any number that doesn't trace to a primary document. "Up to $500 billion" is allowed because the company's own blog says it; a figure from a news summary is not.
      • RULE 5Anything that reads as a response to a breaking story. Rapid response is a communications decision, not a content-generation one.

      What I'd measure

      MetricWhy this one
      Government-pass registrations by office typeThe only number that proves the event reached its audience
      Staffer questions per layerTells you what to write next, which is the real output of an event
      Primary-source click-through on the mobile one-pagerIf they open the FERC post, the explainer did its job
      Share of posts held vs. publishedA healthy policy desk holds things. Zero holds means the rules aren't being applied

      What I chose not to build, and why

      OmissionReason
      Any inferred positionWhere the company has not published a view (federal preemption of state AI laws is the clearest case), the desk says "no published position" and stops. A policy desk that guesses is worse than no desk.
      Rapid responseThe pipeline runs on a daily batch. Responding to a story in the hour is a judgment call for Communications, not a feature.
      Logos, brand assets, or the company's voiceThis is independent work. The drafts borrow the plain, confident register of the company's public blog, nothing more.
      A content audit of existing materialsI did one. It is more useful as a conversation than as a public page.

      What I'd build next

      • State view. The same desk filtered by state: supplier sites, data-center projects, pending state bills, and a one-pager per delegation. The 43-state story is the most under-used asset in the public materials.
      • International layer. The same five layers for the UK, EU and Japan, where the company runs ecosystem events and the policy questions are different (sovereign AI, the AI Act, energy pricing).
      • Staffer-question loop. Feed questions captured at events back into the monitor, so the desk tracks what Washington is asking, not only what agencies are publishing.
      • Open-model drafter. Run the drafting agent on an open-weight model so the desk practices what the Models layer preaches.
