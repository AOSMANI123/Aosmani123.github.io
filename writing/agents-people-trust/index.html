Agents people trust start with one rule

What building a seven-agent system for an executive's office taught me.

Written November 2026Read 3 minutes

Before I designed a single agent, I wrote down one rule: the human is always the final sender. Every other choice in the system came from that sentence.

The system handles requests for an executive's time: speaking invitations, customer meetings, events and media asks. It now manages more than 200 of them. Seven agents share the work, and none of them can send anything to anyone.

One job per agent

Each agent does one thing, and its limits are part of its design:

  • Intake pulls fields out of a request and makes no judgment about priority. Extract, don't interpret.
  • Triage scores each request with a rubric anyone can read. No black-box ranking.
  • Scheduling reads the calendar and proposes a slot. It never books.
  • The briefing drafter cites a source for every claim and flags any section it can't source.
  • The coverage evaluator measures what a briefing missed, not just what it got right.

Narrow jobs made failures easy to find. When a briefing came out wrong, I could tell whether intake misread the request or the drafter reached past its sources. A single do-everything agent would have hidden that.

Trust is a design choice

I built the system to be checked. Scores came with their reasons, claims came with their sources, and every output waited for a person. The result I care about most is in the results table: zero messages sent on their own.

That rule had a cost. A fully autonomous version would have been faster on paper. But an executive's calendar and words are too consequential to hand over, and a system people don't trust doesn't get used at all.

What I chose not to build

Three things I left out on purpose:

  1. A custom model. The value was in the workflow, not the model, and fine-tuning would have added months.
  2. A new interface. The agents work behind the tools the team already used, so nobody had to learn anything new.
  3. Full autonomy. The agents make the team faster, not independent of it.

What carries over

I'd make the same calls anywhere AI drafts something a person signs: a clinical note, a claim, a patient message. Write the rule first. Give each agent one job. Make every output easy to check. Then measure what the system gets wrong, not just how much it handles.
