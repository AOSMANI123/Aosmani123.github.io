Build 01 · Amazon

A seven-agent system for the CEO's engagements

The problem
The CEO's external engagement portfolio runs to 200+ items at a time. Every request needed the same intake, triage and briefing work, done by hand, with no written criteria for what to accept.
My role
I owned the portfolio, and I designed and built the system on Amazon Quick Suite myself. I also wrote the portfolio's first evaluation criteria.
Discovery
25 interviews with product managers, engineering managers and comms and PR leads about where executive workflows actually break.
The tradeoff
The platform could read but not write. So the agents do the drafting and the finding, and a person always makes the final call.

Build 02 · Microsoft

One tracker instead of three manual workflows

Engagement requests for a Corporate Vice President came in through three separate manual workflows. I rebuilt it as one pipeline in Power Automate, AI Builder and SharePoint.

  1. Requests land in one listAI Builder pulls the event, date, requestor and organization out of each email and writes a short summary.
  2. Monday digestOpen requests that need a decision arrive in one email, each with its summary, so nobody has to open every thread.
  3. Drafted declinesLow-fit requests get a polite draft reply. Staff edit and send it themselves.
  4. Briefs for accepted engagementsA second flow pre-assembles the briefing document from the list, ready for review.
80%

less time to update each engagement, and one place to see all of them.

Principles

What I hold to

The human is always the final sender.

AI drafts, sorts and retrieves. A person reads it and decides. Every system I've built works this way.

Write the criteria before you rank anything.

A priority list without criteria is just the loudest request winning. At Amazon, I wrote the evaluation criteria the portfolio had never had.

Put the ask up top.

Busy people read the first line. The decision you need goes there, and the context follows for anyone who wants it.

Don't fill in what you don't know.

In a briefing, a guess dressed up as a fact does more damage than a gap. I flag the gap and go find the answer.
